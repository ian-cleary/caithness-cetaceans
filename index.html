    Species guide — this coast

    Nine regulars: seven cetaceans NatureScot calls relatively common in Scottish waters, plus the two seals. Glyphs are hand-drawn, not photographs.

    Orca Orcinus orca — killer whaleStar of the Firth

    The largest dolphin, and the reason the Pentland Firth is recognised as the UK’s top place to see orcas. Family pods travel the Firth hunting seals, often close inshore — orcas have come within metres of John o’ Groats pier. Named pods pass through, including “the 27s”, watched from Ackergill to Duncansby to John o’ Groats on 7 November 2026.

    When: peak late May–June (Orca Watch), and seen into the autumn.

    Harbour porpoise Phocoena phocoenaMost abundant

    Scotland’s smallest cetacean and the most abundant in inshore waters. Usually alone or in small groups — a small, dark, triangular fin rolling at the surface, no leaping. Common in Dunnet Bay through the summer, following the fish in.

    When: year-round; most often in summer, with an autumn peak in the Shorewatch data.

    Bottlenose dolphin Tursiops truncatusBig & bold

    Big, bold and the most acrobatic of the local dolphins. Eighteen sightings were logged during Orca Watch 2022, and Swiney Head at Lybster is a noted spot for them.

    When: summer peak.

    Common dolphin Delphinus delphisSummer visitor

    Summer visitors, mainly between May and October when food is most abundant. Fast, leaping groups, often well offshore. Six sightings during Orca Watch 2022; one was logged on iNaturalist off this coast on 11 October 2026.

    When: May–October.

    White-beaked dolphin Lagenorhynchus albirostrisNorthern water

    A dolphin of northern waters, listed among the dolphins regularly seen from the Caithness shore. Often in groups, and one of the seven cetaceans NatureScot calls relatively common in Scottish waters.

    When: summer.

    Risso’s dolphin Grampus griseusLate-summer peak

    Big, blunt-headed dolphins, grey and heavily scarred with age. Forty-four sightings were logged during Orca Watch 2022 — as many as minke whales — and one was identified from Dunnet Head in August 2025.

    When: late-summer peak.

    Minke whale Balaenoptera acutorostrataJune–July peak

    The small, fast baleen whale of these waters — a curved back and a small dorsal fin, surfacing for a couple of breaths and gone. Forty-four sightings during Orca Watch 2022. Swiney Head at Lybster is a noted spot.

    When: June–July peak.

    Grey seal Halichoerus grypusYear-round

    The bigger seal, with a long “horse-like” face, and more common than the harbour seal around the Firth. Hauls out in groups on rock platforms — Gills and Brough Bays are the best viewpoints — and on the rocks around Stroma and Swona.

    When: year-round; white pups are born October to January.

    Harbour seal Phoca vitulinaYear-round

    Smaller, round-faced and shyer than the grey. Pups are born in May, June and July and can swim from birth. A good number overwinter on the small islands in the River Thurso, a short walk from the town centre.

    When: year-round; pups May–July.

    Occasional visitors

    • Humpback whale — two sightings during Orca Watch 2022; seen in deeper water.
    • Long-finned pilot whale — regularly seen off this coast, in tight groups.
    • Atlantic white-sided dolphin — recorded off Caithness, often with white-beaked dolphins.
    • Fin whale — the second-largest animal on Earth, in deeper water offshore.
    • Walrus — a rare visitor: one was reported at Reiss Sands, Wick, on 9 October 2026 (Sea Watch Foundation).
    • Basking shark — a fish, not a mammal: the giant filter-feeder passes through the Firth on rare summer days. Report it to the Marine Conservation Society’s Basking Shark Sightings Project.

    Where to watch

    Headlands with height, looking down onto the sea. The Pentland Firth runs from Dunnet Head in the west to Duncansby Head in the east — the strait the orcas travel.

    Dunnet Head

    The most northerly point of mainland Britain, with an RSPB reserve on the cliffs. Seals, dolphins and whales in the Pentland Firth below; the viewing platform is a few metres from the car park.

    Look for: orca, porpoise, minke, seals — and puffins on the cliffs in summer.

    Duncansby Head & Stacks

    Clifftop paths and one of the classic orca vantage points — watchers waited here for pods working up the coast during Orca Watch. About a 15-minute walk from the car park to the stacks viewpoint.

    Look for: orca above all, plus minke, Risso’s and bottlenose dolphins.

    John o’ Groats

    Harbour viewpoints where orcas have come within metres of the pier, and the ferry to Orkney doubles as a watching platform — orca watchers ride it deliberately, and the morning sailing has produced sightings.

    Look for: orca, dolphins, grey seals lounging near the harbour.

    Gills Bay

    Quiet beaches heading west from John o’ Groats towards the ferry pier. Wildlife cruises leave from here, and seals haul out on Stroma and Swona across the Firth.

    Look for: seals on the islands, porpoise, orca and minke in the Firth.

    Brough Bay

    Seals haul out on the rock platforms here, and it is an Orca Watch station — four orcas were watched from here on 24 May 2026.

    Look for: grey and harbour seals hauled out; orca passing through.

    Dunnet Bay

    Two miles of sand backed by dunes, part of the Dunnet Links National Nature Reserve. Porpoises follow the fish in through the summer, and both seal species haul out on the rock platforms.

    Look for: harbour porpoise in summer; seals hauled out at low water.

    Thurso Bay & Scrabster

    Orcas have been spotted from Thurso Bay itself, and harbour seals overwinter on the small islands in the River Thurso. Wildlife cruises and the NorthLink ferry to Stromness leave from Scrabster harbour.

    Look for: harbour seals on the river islands; orca and dolphins from the harbour and the ferry.

    Sandside Head

    Headland above Sandside Bay near Reay. High Life Highland countryside rangers run guided sea watches here during the Sea Watch Foundation’s National Whale & Dolphin Watch in late July.

    Look for: whatever is passing — the rangers bring telescopes.

    Auckengill

    East-coast village on the road to John o’ Groats. The “64s” orca pod came within 30 feet of the shore here in June 2022, and grey seals were logged here in October 2026.

    Look for: orca close inshore; grey seals.

    How to watch

    Reading the sea

    • Choose calm days — white horses hide dorsal fins and blows.
    • Light is best early morning or late afternoon.
    • Pick a headland with height and scan with the naked eye first, then binoculars.
    • Watch the seabirds: flocks working the surface mark food the cetaceans share.
    • If you spot something, search the water to either side — whales travel, and they surface again nearby.
    • Take care on unfenced cliffs.

    After the Caithness Field Club’s cetacean guidance and the Highland Council’s Wild Coastal Trail.

    Report a sighting — or a stranding

    Seen something alive?

    Record the date, time, location, species, number of animals, behaviour — and photographs if you have them. Then:

    • Sea Watch Foundation sighting form — feeds the national database.
    • Log it on the Whale Track app or map.
    • Post in the Caithness & Moray Firth Cetacean Sightings Facebook group — the fastest local pickup.

    A single timestamped photo can identify a known individual or confirm a movement nobody had recorded.

    Found a stranded animal?

    Alive: 01825 765 546

    British Divers Marine Life Rescue, 24 hours a day. The Scottish SPCA helpline is 03000 999 999. Do not try to push the animal back to sea. Keep quiet, keep children and dogs away — noise and crowding cause distress.

    Dead: 07979 245 893

    Scottish Marine Animal Stranding Scheme, or reports@strandings.org. Give the date found, the location (a grid reference if you can), and photographs. Do not handle the animal — wild marine mammals can carry disease.

    Watch by the Scottish Marine Wildlife Watching Code (NatureScot): keep your distance, never chase or crowd an animal, and let it come to you — or not at all.
